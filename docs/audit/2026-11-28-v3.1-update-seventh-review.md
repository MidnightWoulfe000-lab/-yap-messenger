**Verdict: no, the design is not ready to start building.** This review has seven Medium findings and one Low finding; no Critical or High finding. **This is an AI-assisted review, not a professional security audit.** Nothing here establishes that the design is secure, independently reviewed or production-ready. Findings are text/source traces except for the explicitly reported desktop SQLite checks. No Android or physical multi-hop result is claimed.

## Findings

### 1. Medium — the chosen-session guard needs an explicit simultaneous-selection exception

**Locations:** [group encryption](../proposals/group-encryption-v1.md) §3.2, lines 186–191; §3.11, lines 575–590 and 610–620.

**Failure case:** A and B have both failed to read stale monthly setups. A opens catch-up session a to B; B opens b to A. Each has a saved chosen outgoing base key and awaits its first reply. Take A as the smaller identity. The simultaneous rule says a must win. B can authenticate A's setup under a, but it is a message decrypting only under a different session from B's currently chosen b. Applying the new guard literally refuses its commit until a reply on b has been handled. A likewise refuses incoming b while awaiting a. Neither produces the needed committed reply; identical hourly retries cannot change this state.

Selecting a before applying the guard would avoid this trace, but that transition is not stated. The proposal must distinguish a provisional outgoing candidate from the finally selected session and say when authenticated selection may replace it. This is a conflict between two new receive rules, not proof that libsignal itself deadlocks. The pinned library promotes newly processed setups and can promote archived sessions; it does not implement Yap's identity-order choice for it.

**Suggested fix:** order staged authentication, candidate comparison, winner installation, queue retirement, saved response and activation of the guard explicitly. Permit the authenticated winning setup to replace a provisional losing choice before enforcing the guard on other traffic. State the responder's release condition as well as the initiator's first-reply condition. Test both identity orders, both setups in flight, either arrival order, delayed losing-session traffic and restart at each selection/installation boundary. Keep the initial-payload restriction intact and use the pinned library's supported session handling rather than a new cryptographic construction.

### 2. Medium — durable failed-attempt charging conflicts with rolling back every failed operation

**Locations:** group §3.11, lines 636–657; [storage](../proposals/local-storage-v1.md) §4, lines 145–157, and test plan line 504.

**Failure case:** a setup passes the outer and active-member checks, misses the saved-response cache, and is charged one of S's four attempts. Libsignal then rejects its authentication. Storage §4 says each endpoint operation is one transaction, any error aborts it and nothing changes; its test says a failed decrypt leaves every record unchanged. Following that rule rolls back the attempt counter too. Repeated bad setups, especially with a restart between them, therefore do not consume the durable allowance described in the group proposal. Conversely, committing the charge on failure violates the current storage contract unless that exception is specified.

The explicit acceptance of sustained spoofed exhaustion is sensible, but assumes a charge that actually survives failure. It does not answer this persistence boundary. The same issue applies to saving response throttles and other admission bookkeeping when the associated cryptographic operation is refused; those must not be confused with ratchet mutations.

**Suggested fix:** specify the durable admission step separately from the cryptographic transaction, or explicitly define the restricted bookkeeping that a failed operation may commit. Charge before expensive work and do not refund on authentication failure; still discard every session/pre-key/history/outbox mutation from that failed authentication. State what happens if the charge cannot be committed, and how the per-identity/day and phone-wide/hour gates behave across restart. Reconcile §4 and the failed-decrypt test. Test repeated invalid monthly/catch-up/unknown-key inputs with restart immediately after charging and immediately after rejection.

### 3. Medium — a late active sender cannot get its share after the guaranteed total is spent

**Locations:** storage §5, lines 235–244.

**Failure case:** 200 known contact/group senders each use their 60 guaranteed decryptions early in the hour. This has spent exactly 12,000 outside the shared gate. Another known group member sends its first receipt later in that same hour, after the shared gate is also exhausted. At 201 active senders the text reduces everyone's share to 12,000/201, about 59.70. That cannot undo the 12,000 decryptions already performed. Admitting the new sender exceeds the hard guaranteed-total cap; refusing it contradicts its guaranteed share. The app can have more than 200 eligible senders with 128 contacts plus group members, without unknown labels or new group admission.

No rounding, active-set snapshot, redistribution or unused-credit rule is specified. Allocating fresh shares whenever membership in the active set changes can exceed the total; simply reducing remaining shares can starve late senders. These are admission semantics, not a disagreement with 60 or 12,000 as starting values.

**Suggested fix:** define the quota epoch, eligible sender set, integer/fractional credit rule and late-arrival treatment before spending the budget. Choose explicitly between a bounded reservation for all eligible senders, a specified next-epoch admission rule, or another bounded allocation with an honestly narrower guarantee. Do not retroactively subtract work. State how one identity appearing as a contact and in several groups is counted. Test 200 fully served senders followed by the 201st, changing group membership, hour/window boundaries and a configured population larger than the guarantee budget.

### 4. Medium — a copied label can spend another sender's supposedly private work share

**Locations:** storage §5, lines 235–251; [rotating labels](../proposals/rotating-address-labels.md) §§3–5, particularly lines 158–183; group §3.11, lines 716–724.

**Failure case:** a nearby attacker records a genuine current-hour destination label for contact C. The attacker makes 60 distinct, validly stamped envelopes using that destination but junk ciphertext. A label is visible and not an authenticator. The pre-decryption gate attributes each envelope to C and spends C's guaranteed share; the outer decrypt then fails. After other traffic exhausts the shared gate, C's real receipt cannot be classified. The victim's share has been used by someone else. Distinct envelopes avoid ordinary duplicate suppression. A former group member also knows fallback inbox material and can manufacture candidate traffic naming current senders.

An entirely unknown label should not obtain a new sender share, but the text does not state that admission rule either. Even authenticating a group outer layer would prove inbox-key knowledge, not the claimed member's identity. The new guarantee therefore needs a threat-model qualification or a different allocation boundary.

**Suggested fix:** define which admission failures charge which budget, and distinguish untrusted label selection, outer-key knowledge and authenticated inner sender identity. Keep a bounded global path for unauthenticated work; do not give unknown labels free per-sender quotas. Either accept and document that observed-label spoofing can exhaust another sender's share, as with the expressly accepted fake-setup limit, or specify and review the stronger scheduling/authentication property required to avoid it. Do not claim full sender isolation from labels alone. Test copied labels with failed outer tags, known fallback inboxes with forged inner identities, unknown labels and genuine receipts after each budget is exhausted.

### 5. Medium — post-statement WAL growth is not a dirty-page bound

**Locations:** storage §5, lines 188–205 and 217–225.

**Failure case:** a transaction changes more pages than its allowance while those pages remain in SQLite's cache. After its last data statement the WAL can still have grown by zero. The proposal's check passes; COMMIT then writes the dirty pages. Checking after COMMIT cannot roll that successful transaction back. Measuring physical file length is also unreliable when SQLite reuses an existing WAL file, and a large statement can spill well beyond the reserved allowance before an after-statement check gets control.

A desktop check in this review used SQLite 3.54.1, 4,096-byte pages, WAL, FULL synchronisation, disabled autocheckpoint and a 16 MiB cache. After checkpointing to an empty WAL, a transaction inserted a 1 MiB blob. WAL length was **0 before**, **0 after INSERT**, and **1,062,992 after COMMIT**. A secure deletion check had the same delayed-write behaviour (finding 6). These are ordinary SQLite demonstrations, not tests of a pinned SQLCipher/Android build.

[SQLite's WAL documentation](https://www.sqlite.org/wal.html) describes commit records and WAL reuse; its [cache-spill documentation](https://www.sqlite.org/pragma.html#pragma_cache_spill) explains that dirty cache pages may be written during a transaction. Neither supplies the proposed inference from observed file growth to all pages changed.

**Suggested fix:** choose an enforceable bound on dirty pages, WAL frames and statement overshoot before dependent storage work starts. Validate the supported instrumentation or conservative operation bounds in the pinned SQLite/SQLCipher configuration, including cache spill and reused WAL space. Reserve enough physical space for the bounded transaction and its failure path; do not rely on rolling back after commit. Test cached writes, one oversized statement, spill, WAL reuse, rollback, blocked checkpoints and both stores under pressure. The page allowance values can remain experimental; the enforcement mechanism cannot.

### 6. Medium — the initial retirement transaction can exceed its own essential allowance

**Locations:** storage §3, lines 109 and 118–123; §5, lines 195–203; §7, lines 308–324. Code comparison: `endpoint/src/store.rs`, lines 110–116.

**Failure case:** a valid contact has one 512 KiB session, the permitted maximum. Retirement must delete that session, pair/link secrets and cancel the outbox in one “small essential” transaction. With 4 KiB pages, the session alone occupies over 128 pages before indexes, versus an essential allowance of 64. Group departure must delete up to 127 sessions plus pre-keys/inboxes/generations in that same allowance. Correct enforcement refuses retirement indefinitely even with ample free space; retrying after another space check cannot make it fit.

In the desktop SQLite check, deleting a single 524,288-byte session blob with `secure_delete = ON` produced **130 WAL frames** (535,632 bytes including WAL framing), before any of the other retirement work. The post-statement check did not see these until commit, illustrating finding 5 too. This is an example of an admissible record exceeding the transaction contract, not a claim that typical sessions have that size. A large essential session update can encounter the same mismatch.

**Suggested fix:** bound the *entire* atomic retirement operation against the admitted state. One option is a bounded retirement marker that immediately denies all use and invalidates in-memory/send queues, followed by bounded cleanup of session/key blobs as well as history; explicitly disclose that those residual keys remain locally stored until cleanup. If deleting all those secrets at the first commit is required, define how that requirement fits the maximum state and chosen storage/encryption design. Reconcile essential session-update limits too. Test maximum-sized contact sessions, full-group departure, pending outbox reservations and restart while cleanup is paused. This needs a written lifecycle decision, not silent omission of oversized records.

### 7. Medium — the seven-day bootstrap route does not retain its decryption pre-key for seven days

**Locations:** [encrypted pairing](../proposals/encrypted-pairing.md) §5, lines 239–260 and 271–273; group §3.2, lines 172–177, and §3.11, lines 556–559.

**Failure case:** A creates the contact kind 03 from B's current group-log monthly bundle shortly before B replaces that bundle. B keeps the old private pre-keys for 24 hours after replacement and deletes them as specified. A's first kind 03 reaches B 25 hours after replacement, within the signed seven-day friend-request expiry. The new route binding passes the time check, but B lacks the private pre-key needed to open the inner contact setup. A retries exactly those bytes; changing the group's outer piece cannot repair them. This is the newly required delayed-first-bootstrap test at a monthly boundary.

The lifetime catch-up bundle is expressly for catch-up setups. The text does not select it for creating contact sessions, reserve a separate request bundle, pin monthly keys through pending friend requests, or define a replacement contact setup under the same request. Existing group-session recovery is a different session; it does not by itself replace the cached contact bootstrap.

**Suggested fix:** specify which signed bundle contact bootstrap uses and its private-key/replay-state retention through the request deadline, under the existing bounded request admission. Alternatively define an explicit authorised retry/failure transition if a bundle expires, and narrow the seven-day claim accordingly. Use existing reviewed libsignal bundle mechanisms; no new key exchange is needed merely to retain the selected key. Test creation just before monthly replacement, first delivery after retirement, kind 04 loss/restart, crossed requests and final loss of the shared group route. Keep ordinary envelope lifetimes unchanged.

### 8. Low — a few scope and lifecycle summaries remain stale

**Locations and concrete mismatches:**

- Attachments §4, line 186, still says admission applies to both kinds, and lines 192–194 prescribe expiry for incomplete big transfers without marking that clause as later work. An implementer following just this admission list can create a big-transfer lifecycle despite the explicit big-manifest refusal at lines 195–197. Qualify those two references as later-only.
- Attachments test plan, lines 271–273, still says “both size ceilings” are enforced. The current update has one active encrypted-size ceiling. Testing the future 100 MiB acceptance boundary here contradicts the explicit refusal of all big manifests. Separate current refusal tests from future-channel tests.
- Storage test plan, lines 513–515, says removing a contact leaves no record and a crash leaves no orphan/dangling reference after startup. The new retirement deliberately leaves hidden rows and pending cleanup, and explicitly permits cleanup to pause for low space. Qualify the physical-deletion assertion by completed cleanup; at retirement/startup test immediate denial of use and durable cleanup intent instead.

**Suggested fix:** make these scope and completion qualifications explicit. The detailed no-big-transfer rule and resumable retirement rule should control implementation in the meantime. These are Low documentation defects, not a request to review attachments §3 or redesign the deferred channel.

## Detailed trace assessments

### Catch-up, saved responses and work order

For a single recovery, a lost initial setup leaves the initiator's exact bytes/deadline pending. Once received, the atomic installation/reply record supports restart before response publication. A lost reply is answered from that record, including with a different valid outer wrapper and when the 256-slot replay list is full. The digest is over the exact inner setup, so rewrapping does not defeat the lookup. A forged different setup cannot gain a match merely by copying a member identity; a matching SHA-256 digest is being used as an exact-byte cache identifier, not as a new authentication primitive.

A member or former member who captured the exact accepted inner setup and can supply an admissible outer layer may trigger the cached encrypted reply. This is an intentional replay response, rate-limited to once an hour per member and subject to the stated overall catch-up-answer ceiling. It must go back to the original member/session/inbox, never an address supplied by the replaying frame. The cache hit changes no session, consumes no replay slot and must be scoped to the same group, local/remote identities and membership context as the accepted record. It must not extend the seven-day deadline. This lookup belongs after outer and current-membership checks, as the new work order says, rather than before all validation.

After cache expiry, an already used base is not accepted as a fresh setup. If its session remains, libsignal's duplicate handling still applies; if the session is gone, the durable used-base record refuses reconstruction. Neither an unmatched duplicate error nor a full-list refusal proves acceptance. New authenticated bases still consume permanent slots; invalid setups cannot fill another member's list. The 256-slot no-eviction policy remains a valid fail-closed availability limit, not a promise of unlimited recovery.

The four-attempt per-identity counter now covers monthly/catch-up/unknown IDs across groups, and the text openly permits indefinite targeted exhaustion under continued spoofing. That limit can stay. Finding 2 concerns whether failed attempts are durably charged at all, not a demand for Sybil-proof recovery. Finding 1 concerns the ordering of selection and the guard when both readable catch-ups already exist; the ordinary lost-reply counterexample from round six is no longer being repeated as an unfixed defect.

The first-payload rule remains suitably narrow: only signed public bundle material and log heads before the reply on the selected session; no sender keys, missing log events or old queued pieces. The capture/deletion/later-compromise experiment and separate composition review are still needed to establish which later traffic retained catch-up secrets can open. This review neither demonstrates historical group-chat decryption from those secrets alone nor proves later confidentiality.

### Guaranteed work, space and retirement

For at most 200 correctly attributed senders with unused shares, the arithmetic is 200 × 60 = 12,000 guaranteed decryptions plus at most 6,000 shared, **18,000/hour**, or five per second on average. Beyond its 60, one sender may use up to 2,000 shared attempts, giving 2,060 for that sender if shared capacity remains. The three-contacts × 2,000 test consumes only 5,820 shared attempts after their guaranteed 180; it does **not** quite exhaust the 6,000 shared gate. Use three × 2,060, or specify a different ordering, when testing actual exhaustion. Ordinary and essential commit limits remain separate at 3,000/hour each globally, and 1,000 per sender for each class. A sender's unused decrypt share cannot guarantee successful commit if the essential global quota, byte reserve, page allowance or physical floor has independently been exhausted.

The new totals are work ceilings, not battery or space measurements. If each admitted envelope were 64 KiB, 18,000 attempts represent about **1.10 GiB/hour** of candidate envelope bytes presented for classification, before ratchet/PQ costs. Existing native and mesh limits may reduce that traffic and are not bypassed. Failed decrypts should not create history/session state; admission counters themselves need bounded storage. Successfully retained records consume the 2 GB/5 GB quotas and carrier caps, not an unbounded hourly allocation. No joule, CPU-time or SQLCipher-write estimate follows from the count. Measure a full-hour mix of failed outer, failed inner, normal, hand-over and new-session inputs on real phones; counts alone cannot support “cheap”. Findings 3–4 address admission semantics independently of the numerical tuning.

The tier lines and cleanup threshold now distinguish ordinary work, essentials and reclamation. Cleanup can proceed below the 1 GB headroom to 64 MB free, with two allowances of headroom required for its transaction; below that it pauses visibly. At exactly the threshold there is no room for an additional reserved write. These are honest operational limits if enforcement is fixed. A retirement marker must govern every app read/send/receive, pending worker job, attachment publish, label table and recognition generation after commit and after restart. Remaining encrypted history is deliberately still in the database until cleanup. The two-part plan should not promise physical erasure of flash or an old copied store. Findings 5–6 are why the current enforcement and first transaction are not yet ready to implement.

### Attachment states and accounting

The current attachment lifecycle has defined recovery actions:

| Durable state or crash point | Required action and charge |
| --- | --- |
| Receiving, bytes written but range not committed | Re-receive the unrecorded range; retain the full single reservation. |
| Receiving, range committed after file sync | Preserve that range; ACK only after commit. If the whole staging file is missing, recreate it and clear ranges. |
| Checking | Return to Receiving and recheck all present bytes; never publish on an unfinished check. The charge remains. |
| Before Resetting commit | Old complete ranges/file still exist; validation repeats after restart. |
| Resetting committed, before/after truncation and sync | Ranges already cleared; empty/sync again idempotently, then commit Receiving. A restart must not increment the validation-failure count again just for completing the same intent. |
| Publishing committed, final file exists | Complete Published using the recorded final name. |
| Publishing, only staging file exists | Rename to the recorded final name, sync the folder, then complete Published. |
| Publishing, neither file exists | Recreate empty Receiving with no ranges; keep the charge. |
| Published | Convert reservation classification to file classification without adding a second charge. Missing file is shown as missing. |
| Deleting | Finish file deletion and remove the durable row/mark; keep its charge until the row is gone. |

The sweep protects both intended names of a Publishing row and all other live references before deleting unreferenced files. Unclean shutdown during publication must not invent another random final name. Resetting/Publishing/Deleting operations are completed before the app sends or shows results; failures should preserve the durable intent for a later retry. The second validation failure takes the existing Deleting/error path, not another successful publication. These statements follow the combined state/charge rules; no power-loss execution has validated them. Files missing through external corruption can lose progress, but must not become falsely acknowledged ranges.

One charge per live row now avoids both double-counting a completed file and losing its reservation during Checking/Publishing. Small attachments remain capped at 262,144 encrypted bytes, with at most five 60 KiB transport pieces. For plaintext sizes 0, 1, 65,535, 65,536, 65,537 and 262,080, the segment/encrypted-size pairs remain (1,16), (1,17), (1,65,551), (1,65,552), (2,65,569) and (4,262,144). A 262,081-byte plaintext is over the small ceiling after tags. This is arithmetic, not STREAM interoperability. Delaying direct transfer removes the need to implement its range encodings now; it does not remove crash-safe staging for carried small pieces.

### Friend-bootstrap binding and Ready behaviour

The route binding is distinct in purpose and bytes from the ordinary envelope binding: a fixed friend-bootstrap domain label plus the stored request ID and its signed expiry, inside authenticated plaintext. Acceptance must require this route, the expected kind/role and the stored winning request; an ordinary-envelope parser must never fall back to it to avoid expiry. Changing request ID/expiry without valid inner authentication fails; an authentic bootstrap from another request fails the stored-request match. Each retry can use a newly dated group outer carrier while preserving exact inner bootstrap bytes. Canonical lengths/encoding and no-trailing-byte vectors should be fixed in the first implementation tests.

A Ready responder now retains and replays kind 04 until the original signed expiry. Exact kind 03 replays must only schedule that cached response: they neither reopen a session nor count as proof that the initiator received kind 04. In particular the “Finishing” warning should clear on subsequent contact-session progress proving the peer advanced, not on replay of the bootstrap which created that warning. A repeated group-layer wrapper also does not prove contact Ready; it authenticates a separate group session.

Each phone checks signed expiry against its own working time, so one can expire earlier and cancel while the other keeps its Ready-but-unconfirmed contact. The explicit replacement rule at lines 264–269 is a special case of the otherwise general “already friends: ignore” rule and should be treated as such. A new replacement request still requires its own consent, request binding, contact/session retirement and secret selection; it must not turn an arbitrary request into silent replacement of an established, confirmed friendship. Losing the last shared group prevents further group-carried bootstrap; before Ready the attempt cancels, while a Ready responder may remain in the disclosed unconfirmed state. These are limitations of asymmetric completion, not a proof of delivery. Finding 7 is the remaining private-key-lifetime defect even with these route rules enforced.

### Waiting line, recognition and hiding

The waiting line can accumulate at most the next whole offer's required credit, never partial WANT service. With no service by others while a head waits, sufficient credit for a fixed offer of at most 64 entries becomes available when earlier rolling-minute debits expire, within about a minute. Then a silent head occupies at most its ten-second ready window before moving back. A head closing releases the credit immediately; a served head leaves and later rejoins at the back if busy. A hostile sender changing offers must not reset an already-running turn timer or exceed the 64-entry ceiling. Under finite connected links and retry timers this is implementable best-effort service. Malicious reconnects can still slow useful service, and no delivery or speed bound follows. The listed deterministic scheduler tests have not run here.

Recognition now only names a row. It starts no transfer, carries no permission and makes no claim about physical distance. The generation/week check and filler fallback still answer the stale-worker/queued-probe/immediate-reconnect cases; hiding retains incoming matching material and invalidates outgoing real tags before new links can use them. Whole-exchange timing, asynchronous write cancellation, table publication and shuffling remain unmeasured. Hiding continues to allow ordinary messages and small attachment pieces: neither their addressing nor reassembly depends on recognition. Messages known to a seized friend and general traffic observations remain the stated privacy limits; removing direct transfer does not prove that nothing can reveal a change in behaviour.

### Clock and the one-hour exception

Let E be the endpoint's durable floor and C a later floor used by a carrier-only operation. The new authority rule enforces C ≤ E + one hour before returning C. If saving a required newer endpoint floor fails, the authority cannot return a value beyond that bound or run dependent expiry/label work using it. Startup opens/reconciles both stores before those operations, selecting max(E,C); if the carrier must be recreated, only E survives. The resulting backward step is at most one hour for that recreation. A crash between endpoint and carrier commits is safe for this bound because the endpoint commit was required first for a larger advance.

A weekly secret step/deletion is an endpoint transaction which also saves its working time. Recreating just the carrier therefore cannot roll the committed secret state back, nor restore an expired/deleted endpoint row. It can extend the remaining acceptance window for old traffic near expiry or delay time-based cleanup by the lost hour. Tombstones retained until 30 days after friend-request expiry do not disappear merely because the clock fell back an hour, so this is not a route to replaying a months-old friend request. A repeated series of local carrier losses can cause repeated bounded setbacks; “up to an hour” is a bound on each loss, not a total lifetime drift bound. Both-store restoration remains the separate admitted rollback limit.

One friend, two colluding friends or a stranger cannot directly exercise this exception: their time replies only cause a note and cannot delete a carrier key or lower the wall clock. Traffic replay near a genuine local recreation can encounter its extended window, subject to dedup, working time and exact expiry checks. A hostile OS/file writer or a user confirming a false forward jump is a different admitted threat. The serial authority, saturating arithmetic and floor-before-use order now state a coherent policy. Validate it on restart/key-loss tests; this review has not run that policy in an implementation.

## Ready to start building?

**No.** The remaining blockers are the written decisions in findings 1–7:

1. Order authentication, simultaneous winner selection and the chosen-session guard so the guard cannot block installing the winner.
2. Give failed-attempt admission charges a durable commit boundary consistent with cryptographic rollback.
3. Specify guaranteed-share epochs/eligible sets and late arrivals after the 12,000 total is spent.
4. State and enforce the attribution boundary for those shares, or explicitly accept observed-label spoofing as a limit and narrow the guarantee.
5. Replace post-statement WAL-file growth with a demonstrated physical write-bound mechanism.
6. Reconcile atomic retirement/essential updates with all allowed session and group state sizes.
7. Keep the selected contact-bootstrap pre-key available for the request's advertised window, or specify a reviewed expiry/retry limit.

Correct the Low residual summaries in finding 8. These are concrete contract conflicts or missing transitions; they are not requests for a redesign of already accepted numerical starting values.

**Values and stated limits that can stay:** copy counts, stamp difficulty, headroom/tier lines, the 64 MB logical reserve, byte shares, page allowance numbers, 60/12,000 guaranteed work totals, shared/commit rates, offer timers and the 256-record cap can remain measured starting values once the rules governing them are coherent. Restored-store rollback, copied devices, sponsor-log completeness, sustained flooding, indefinitely spoofed setup exhaustion and the per-recreation one-hour clock exception can remain explicit limits. No promise of starvation-free adversarial delivery or physical closeness is needed. The unwritten big-attachment channel is out of scope and no longer blocks this update.

After the text is settled, the first implementation work must pin/check dependencies and run isolated feasibility tests before dependent integration: `snow = 0.10.0` on Android with pairing/resume vectors and malformed-frame limits; a specific `rusqlite`/SQLCipher/crypto-provider combination with raw-key, HMAC, FULL/WAL, actual page-bound and key-loss/startup tests; a compatible pinned `aead-stream`/`chacha20poly1305` family with boundary/final-flag vectors; and libsignal at the existing pin with durable last-resort replay, simultaneous/lost-reply recovery, chosen-session handling and the capture/deletion/later-compromise experiment. The group-state shortcut must remain disabled until compared with full derivation. Passing these tests would be evidence for those contracts, not a security audit. Independent expert review before sensitive use remains separately outstanding.
