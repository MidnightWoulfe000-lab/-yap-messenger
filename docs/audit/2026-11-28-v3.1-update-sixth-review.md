# Sixth review of the v3.1 update

**Reviewer:** Codex, at the project owner's request.  
**Date:** 28 November 2026.  
**Revision:** HEAD `8953fcd22b5cf1d32a3abba7ae401a223a200ef0`. The proposals are not implemented. Locations below refer to the files read at that revision; the review request itself was untracked.

**Verdict: no, the integrated design is not ready to start building.** One High, seven Medium and one Low finding remain in this review; no Critical finding. The sending-generation rule, bounded pairing lookup, batch validation and narrower catch-up exposure statement answer substantial parts of round five. However, a lost catch-up reply still lacks an idempotent recovery path, essential traffic can exhaust its gate before classification, and attachment reset/publication cannot recover as specified across a crash. Setup admission, group-carried bootstrap, low-space writes, waiting-line transitions and loss of the carrier's clock floor also need explicit decisions. Keep the implementation hold. Nothing in this report is approved for building.

**This is an AI-assisted review, not a professional security audit.** No independent expert review, production readiness, Android result or physical multi-hop validation is claimed. Failure traces below are reasoning from text and source, unless explicitly identified as executed checks.

## Findings

### 1. High — a lost catch-up reply has no durable repeat-response path

**Locations:** [group encryption](../proposals/group-encryption-v1.md) §3.11, lines 563–595 and 610–633; §3.6, lines 356–369; [storage](../proposals/local-storage-v1.md) §3, lines 122–123. Code comparison: `endpoint/src/engine.rs`, lines 637–642 and 754–779; pinned libsignal `session_management.rs`, `try_decrypt_from_record()`.

**Failure case:** R sends its limited setup to S. S authenticates it and commits the new session/base-key record, then sends its reply. The reply is lost. R follows the new rule and resends the identical setup an hour later. S has already consumed that inner message: libsignal refuses its duplicate. The accepted-piece cache in §3.6 records hand-over generations and their generation acknowledgements; it does not define a catch-up-setup record or the exact first reply to resend. A used-base-key entry proves prior use, not durable acceptance of this payload or which reply was generated. Repeating R's hourly send therefore does not itself recover the exchange. A restart after S's installation but before sending the reply exposes the same missing durable output.

The existing endpoint explicitly caches bootstrap responses before calling libsignal on duplicates. The new catch-up exchange needs an equivalent stated contract. Applying the setup allowance or full replay-list refusal before an accepted-response lookup can also reject a legitimate retry, including the retry of the 256th accepted setup.

**Suggested fix:** define a bounded, durable catch-up transaction containing session installation, failed-queue retirement, authenticated setup digest, replay record, exact reply bytes and retry phase/deadline. An authenticated exact repeat should schedule the saved reply under a bounded response rate without creating a session or consuming another replay slot. State rewrapping and record retention through the retry window, and what happens after that record expires. Never turn an arbitrary duplicate error into proof of acceptance. Test loss of the setup, loss of the reply, repeated hints and restart before/after installation and reply publication for both identity orders and simultaneous catch-up.

### 2. Medium — the failed-setup work order excludes the stale monthly setup that triggers recovery

**Locations:** group §3.2, lines 172–182; §3.11, lines 559–620 and 622–637.

**Failure case:** the new pre-libsignal sequence starts “for every incoming set-up that names a catch-up pre-key”. The trigger described immediately above is instead a message naming a retired monthly pre-key. For ordinary monthly keys, §3.2 still describes four sessions *accepted* per member per day. A former member who knows R's fallback inbox can submit structurally valid setups claiming S, naming a monthly key R still retains, but failing inner authentication. These are not catch-up-key setups and never become accepted sessions. Following the text literally permits them to bypass both the new failed-attempt charge and its 64-attempt hourly gate, up to the separate general ingress/work limits. The response claims the broader failed-setup order as fixed.

Even on the covered path, “wait up to a day” is only true after the attacker stops: the same four spoofed attempts can be sent every new allowance period. The honest member can remain blocked indefinitely under sustained attack. That is an availability limit, not a replay bypass. Properly authenticated successful setups alone should consume permanent used-base-key slots; spoofed identities must not fill another member's lifetime list.

**Suggested fix:** apply one explicit pre-PQ attempt gate to monthly and catch-up setups, including missing/unknown pre-key IDs and failures, with the accepted-repeat exception in finding 1. State the scope of per-member counters across groups and their restart behaviour. Preserve the chosen no-eviction cap and fail-closed write rule. Describe recurrent spoofed exhaustion as a sustained denial-of-service limit, or specify a reviewed admission mechanism with stronger availability; do not imply identity authentication before the inner message succeeds. Add monthly-key failures to the existing exhaustion/restart tests.

### 3. Medium — ordinary traffic can still close the gate to essentials

**Locations:** storage §5, lines 212–246, especially admission steps 1 and 3 and the claim at lines 224–225.

**Failure case:** one contact sends 2,000 authentic ordinary envelopes in an hour. The first 1,000 use its ordinary allowance; the next 1,000 are decrypted and refused at classification. All 2,000 use the work gate. Its next valid receipt is refused before classification even though it has used none of its essential allowance. Three such senders can exhaust the global 6,000 gate and block a fourth contact's receipt or a justified group key change. The same counterexample works with the 2 GB logical limit reached or in the third physical tier: reserving bytes does not reserve classification work.

This is a finite burst using the proposed quotas, not a claim that unlimited hostile load can always be served. The narrower claim that a receipt passes immediately after *exactly* 1,000 ordinary messages is true; “while the same contact floods” is not. The later generic “Rates” paragraph also needs to defer explicitly to the separate essential budget.

**Suggested fix:** choose the availability contract. Either state that essential commit quotas are separate but classification can still be denied by unclassified traffic, and revise the promises/tests accordingly, or specify a bounded scheduling/admission rule that actually reserves the required essential opportunity. A larger common gate alone does not supply that property. Do not add an unlimited decrypt bypass or trust a clear unauthenticated “essential” flag. Test at 1,000 and 2,000 per sender and at the global work ceiling, with both logical and physical pressure.

### 4. Medium — logical new bytes do not bound the physical cost of updates and deletion

**Locations:** storage §5, lines 176–208 and 212–215; §4, lines 145–156; §7, lines 277–284.

**Failure case:** at free space `1 GB + 33 MB`, remove a contact with hundreds of megabytes of history. §7 requires the contact, session, history and outbox removal in one transaction. Its new logical bytes can be near zero or negative, so the doubled-new-bytes test admits it as shrinking. With `secure_delete = ON`, the operation can dirty many existing database pages and append them to the WAL before commit. The post-commit 16/64 MB checkpoint checks cannot bound that transaction's peak. There is also no exact growth allowance for a permitted essential update in this tier: changing a small record can write several pages and indexes. Below the last line database deletions are prohibited, while attachment deletion starts with a database mark; the recovery path for deleting those referenced files is not reconciled.

The explicit incremental-vacuum choice is sound as a reclamation operation: [SQLite documents](https://www.sqlite.org/pragma.html#pragma_incremental_vacuum) removal of up to N freelist pages. It does not bound the earlier deletion transaction or make file deletion and database deletion the same operation.

**Suggested fix:** distinguish logical quota deltas from a conservative physical dirty-page/WAL allowance for every write class, including updates, deletes, accounting and reclamation. Give the atomic contact-removal requirement an implementable low-space strategy, for example logical retirement followed by bounded, durable cleanup batches, with its visibility/security rules reviewed. Define how cleanup starts at the lowest tier and how both stores reserve shared physical headroom. Keep failed commits unacknowledged and explicitly allow a visible pause if cleanup cannot progress. Test a large existing chat deletion near each line, rather than only inserting new records there.

### 5. Medium — attachment reset and publication still lack crash-safe intermediate states

**Locations:** [attachments](../proposals/attachments-v1.md) §2, lines 94–99; §4, lines 172–201; storage §7a, lines 292–315.

**Failure case A, reset:** all ranges have been committed, final validation fails, and the staging file is truncated. Power fails before the database range reset commits. The durable row is still Checking (or Receiving if that transition was committed first). Startup returns Checking to Receiving and preserves the existing file's recorded ranges. It now claims bytes that truncation removed, contradicting the synced-range invariant. “Emptied and ... reset in one step” cannot be one atomic operation across the file and database. Reversing that order needs a rule too: stale bytes must never make a reset transfer look complete.

**Failure case B, publication:** validation succeeds while the row is Checking. The staging file is renamed to its final random name and the directory synced. Power fails before committing Published. The database still names the staging file, while the final file can look orphaned to the startup sweep. Checking is sent back to Receiving, but that branch has no explicit missing-staging-file repair or recovery of the validated final name. Accepted data can be deleted or left in an unspecified state. The general “orphan file” rule for a new addition does not define recovery of an existing transfer row.

Finished files are correctly excluded from unfilled reservations in principle. However, reservation/actual-byte accounting must remain charged throughout Checking and publication; “Receiving rows only” is insufficient without an explicit transition to charged file bytes in those intermediate states.

**Suggested fix:** define durable reset/publication intent, including old/new names, range invalidation, validation-failure count and quota ownership. State the idempotent startup action after each file operation and each database commit. Keep acknowledged ranges tied to durable bytes, keep every incomplete transfer charged until publication/deletion, and protect intended final files from the orphan sweep. Test power loss before and after truncation, range reset, rename, directory sync, Published commit and reservation conversion, including two failed validations. The ordinary write → sync → range commit → ACK order is otherwise a useful fix.

### 6. Medium — group-friend bootstrap retries stop too early and inherit the wrong lifetime

**Locations:** [encrypted pairing](../proposals/encrypted-pairing.md) §5, lines 239–248 and 263–286; §3, lines 165 and 187–193; [endpoint contract](../android-endpoint-v1.md), “Messages and receipts” and “Reviewed state-machine and resource details”.

**Failure case:** B is the larger identity. B accepts kind 03, becomes Ready and emits kind 04, which is lost. A remains in Bootstrap. §5 says B answers a repeated kind 03 with cached kind 04, but also says both stop at Ready or expiry, whichever comes first. It never explicitly gives a Ready group-friend responder the continued response window that §3 gives QR pairing. There is no group-friend rule for the resulting asymmetric contact if A reaches expiry and deletes its finishing contact while B remains Ready.

There is a second lifetime boundary: the existing kind 03/04 ciphertexts authenticate an envelope binding and expire in 24 hours; the bootstrap cache is retained through that expiry. Merely carrying those exact bytes as a group piece does not make them acceptable for seven days. If A's first bootstrap delivery is delayed 25 hours, or its cached reply is needed then, an implementer must choose which inner expiry/cache checks to change. Outer-piece rewrapping changes neither the authenticated inner binding nor the contact readiness deadline.

**Suggested fix:** specify the group-carried bootstrap representation and lifetime separately from QR transport and today's endpoint envelope. Keep exact inner retries and cached responses valid for the chosen request window, with newly dated outer carriers as necessary, without silently extending ordinary envelope lifetimes. Explicitly allow a Ready responder to answer through the agreed deadline and state the one-side-Ready status/re-pair rule at expiry. Test lost kind 04 plus responder restart, first kind 03 delivery after 24 hours, expiry on one phone first, and loss of the last shared group.

### 7. Medium — the waiting line omits service-completion and insufficient-credit transitions

**Locations:** [mesh v3.1](../proposals/local-mesh-v3.1-group-envelopes.md) §1.4, state table line 320 and budget lines 374–388.

**Failure case:** twelve links offer 64 entries each. Nine can consume 576 entries, leaving 24. The next link queues and retries every five seconds. Its offer cannot fit, but it does retry within ten seconds, so the only specified demotion condition (“does not retry”) never applies. The text does not say whether the ten-second hold expires, restarts on each insufficient retry, or waits until 64 credits are available. Once it eventually receives an answered WANT, the text also never says whether it leaves the line or moves to the back. Leaving it at the front gives it first use again and can stall the rest. The earlier state table independently schedules busy retries at 30, 60, 120 seconds, which miss a ten-second turn unless the five-second rule explicitly overrides it.

**Suggested fix:** give the queue event transitions: when a turn starts, how credit accumulates for a whole offer, what happens at its deadline with insufficient credit, how a successful answer removes/rotates the link, and which retry timer applies while queued. State assumptions for eventual service (continued link availability, timers and sufficient released credit). “Best effort” is appropriate for speed and hostile churn; it does not choose these transitions. Exercise the twelve-offer case and arrivals/departures with a deterministic scheduler before using a fairness claim.

### 8. Medium — recreating the carrier can lose the only committed clock floor

**Locations:** storage §3, lines 131–139; §8, lines 336–342 and 363–377.

**Failure case:** the endpoint has committed floor T. The serial authority accepts wall time T+36 hours and a carrier-only operation commits that value in the carrier database. No endpoint transaction occurs. The wall clock is corrected back to T and the process restarts after the carrier key becomes unreadable. The specified empty-carrier recovery deletes that database and copies the endpoint's floor T. A previously *committed* working time is now lost, despite no restoration of an older endpoint database. Centralising calculation and choosing the later readable floor do not recover an unreadable floor. The text only discloses loss of carried envelopes for this recovery.

**Suggested fix:** decide whether the authoritative floor must be durably advanced in the endpoint before a carrier-only operation can use a later value, or whether loss of the carrier explicitly weakens the monotonicity guarantee to the highest surviving readable commit. Specify that exception and its effects on expiry/secret schedules if accepted. Keep startup expiry/pruning/labels blocked until reconciliation completes. Test carrier-only time advance followed by key loss/recreation, separately from ordinary split commits and restoring both stores. No new cryptographic construction is needed.

### 9. Low — several acceptance tests still demand incompatible behaviour

**Locations:** pairing test plan, lines 349–354; [labels](../proposals/rotating-address-labels.md) §2, lines 140–143 and test lines 261–272; group §6, lines 1061–1062 and §7, lines 1073–1076; mesh tests, lines 900–902; storage §8, lines 371–372 and 383–384.

**Failure case:** a friend request loses one shared group but retains another. The detailed pairing rule continues it; the test still says leaving the group before Ready cancels. A QR link lost before Confirmed is cancelled by pairing, whereas labels §2 broadly says a link closing before Ready may resume and its later test repeats that wording. A test following either summary can reject an implementation following the detailed lifecycle. Mesh's test still says an envelope already held is never offered, although forgetting settled state explicitly permits re-offers. Group's restored-backup test promises erased keys cannot return, contradicting the admitted rollback limit. Its order of work still refers to separation from a legacy chat that has already been removed.

**Suggested fix:** qualify the summaries/tests with Confirmed/Ready, last-shared-group and remembered-cache conditions. Replace the impossible backup assertion with a test demonstrating and documenting the accepted restored-store limit. Remove the stale legacy-chat implementation step. These are documentation corrections, not requests to rebuild the out-of-scope clean-ups.

## Assessment of the requested traces

### Catch-up selection, first payload and replay state

An unreadable monthly setup no longer wins merely because its initiator has the smaller identity. In either identity order the reverse readable catch-up is selected, with installation/queue retirement/base-key recording committed together. For simultaneous readable catch-ups the intended smaller-identity choice is now explicit. However, it is a Yap rule to enforce, not libsignal's automatic tie-break: the pinned `session.rs` promotes a newly processed incoming state, and `session_management.rs` can promote a successfully decrypted archived session. Integration must preserve the selected session and associate its first reply with that session under reorderings. The size experiment exercises none of this. The required simultaneous/loss/restart tests remain necessary; finding 1 identifies a definite missing repeat-response contract independently of those tests.

The payload restriction now makes a useful, testable boundary: only public bundle material and log-head hashes may precede the reply on a fresh catch-up session; no sender keys, log events or queued application pieces. Retries must not append further pre-reply payloads. This supports the narrower *stated cost*, not proof of confidentiality of subsequent traffic. Keep the pinned-library capture/deletion/later-compromise experiment and separate cryptographic composition review outstanding. This review neither demonstrates retrospective group-chat decryption from those retained pre-keys nor establishes its impossibility.

The 256-record rule refuses further fresh catch-ups safely rather than evicting replay protection. Filling one's own list deliberately, or eventually through genuine recovery, can force the chosen re-invitation outcome. A forged setup must not occupy another member's permanent list because its identity is not yet authenticated. At four successful new bases per day, 256 slots can fill in 64 days; “rare” is an expectation, not a bound. About 20 KB per peer and 2.6 MB for 127 peers are plausible payload estimates, before SQLCipher/index overhead. Membership churn and multiple groups also consume the overall group-byte quota; the current 128-member count is not a lifetime disk bound. Never reclaim used-base records while their reusable key and an admissible membership still permit the corresponding replay.

### Recognition and pairing dispatch

With the specified generation/week predicate enforced at send permission, a stalled worker, stale queued calculation, immediate reconnect, contact deletion and week-boundary table all get filler rather than a withdrawn real tag. A stale table published later remains unusable because its generation is wrong. Matching remains available while hiding. The four visibility combinations are coherent: each side can recognise the other only if the other sends a real tag and it retains the matching secret. Big transfers additionally require local permission at both ends and mutual recognition, so hiding pauses them; ordinary messages and small carried attachments continue.

These are text-level conclusions. An implementation must serialise the final permission check with hiding and link closure so an asynchronously queued write cannot bypass it. Timing of the entire exchange, filler generation/replacement, shuffling and cancellation remains unmeasured; fixed HMAC counts do not prove indistinguishability.

Retained local commitments now allow a Ready responder to dispatch a resume after restart with no QR displayed. Opening a new QR preserves up to two old attempts; the third Confirmed attempt ends the oldest. A frame with no matching discovery binding does no handshake work. An unsolicited valid-length handshake on a bound link selects only that attempt, under its stated link/start caps; a public commitment is a selector, not authentication. The PSK handshake supplies authentication. The detailed dispatch still needs state-transition tests during Handshake/Exchange, which are intermediate states of the original handshake, rather than selecting a second protocol in the middle of it.

The 48-byte empty-payload NNpsk0 handshake messages are consistent with a 32-byte ephemeral key and 16-byte authentication tag. [Noise revision 34](https://noiseprotocol.org/noise.html#message-format) limits complete messages to 65,535 bytes, including the tag; plaintext transport capacity is therefore at most 65,519 bytes. This is a specification/arithmetic check, not a `snow` interoperability result.

### Friend requests and batches

The signed pair-wide request resolves crossed requests even through different groups: the smaller identity's immutable request wins, and its secret/week alone are installed. Losing accepts cannot change that selection. Each received route must still be authorised by the receiver's current group state. Leaving one shared group preserves another route; losing the last before Ready cancels. A request first delivered six days later retains the original signed expiry rather than getting seven fresh days. Replaying it after the 30-day tombstone has gone fails its expiry check, subject to the disclosed clock-floor/rollback limits. These conclusions assume the receiver enforces the declared exact seven-day created/expiry relationship and canonical signed fields. Finding 6 concerns the subsequent bootstrap, not those corrected bindings.

The batch layout now permits a straightforward bounded parser: validate the complete directory and checked lengths/hours/unique labels before slicing; scan at most 128 labels; charge every candidate outer opening to the same 32-attempt budget. Thirty-three candidates do not permit a 33rd decryption: stop, retain if at least one piece opened, otherwise directory-only admission is “not for me”. Matching the current group destination independently allows retention of a structurally valid batch, including one with no local piece. Inner failure does not delete that batch. This deliberately permits current-label junk with a valid container to be carried; membership/outer knowledge is not proof of a valid hand-over. The limits bound work, not useful-piece delivery against malicious directory contents. Successful opened bytes held only in memory must be recovered/reprocessed under a defined worker attempt after restart; there is no evidence of implementation here.

### Storage, clock, attachment arithmetic and size claims

Storage and attachments agree on 10,000 carried envelopes and 64 MiB of envelope bytes, excluding database overhead; neither raises the native 1 MiB/minute intake bound. Incremental reclamation and the no-full-VACUUM rule now agree. Logical quotas, physical headroom and essential rates are different limits; findings 3–5 identify where their transitions still disagree.

The ordered working-time calculation preserves an accepted in-run advance, saturates arithmetic and has one serial authority. Startup waits for both stores or explicit carrier recreation, then selects their readable saved maximum. A normal split commit preserves the later saved value. Uncommitted returned times need not survive power loss, now explicitly admitted. One friend, two colluding friends or a stranger cannot directly advance it through messages: link time replies only cause a warning. An attacker controlling the system clock or persuading the user to confirm a false jump is a separate admitted limit. Finding 8 concerns losing a committed floor during permitted recreation, not the earlier in-run arithmetic defect. Re-pairing does not repair global time.

The final flag belongs to the authenticated STREAM nonce selection, not an extra file byte. A maximal small encrypted file needs five 60 KiB pieces; segment and transport-piece boundaries deliberately differ. This checks arithmetic, not STREAM interoperability or decoder safety. Sync-before-range-commit handles a crash after a chunk write or range commit; finding 5 concerns the later destructive/reset/publication operations.

The size source counts 72 bytes around each libsignal message and five bytes once per batch. For the reported 2,582-byte sample, 126 pieces total 325,332 bytes, beyond five 57,344-byte batch budgets (286,720), requiring at least six batches per sender and 762 across 127 senders. This arithmetic supports the revised table. Actual encoded bytes remain the admission rule; sampled maxima do not prove all possible session states fit. [SQLCipher's API documentation](https://www.zetetic.net/sqlcipher/sqlcipher-api/#cipher_integrity_check) confirms `cipher_integrity_check`, added in 4.2.0, checks page HMACs and reports errors. That resolves the name-from-memory concern; it does not verify an as-yet-unpinned Android configuration or replace logical database integrity checks.

## Ready to start building?

**No.** The remaining design blockers are the transitions or failure contracts in findings 1–8:

1. Durable catch-up setup/reply replay, installation and retry state, including the full-list accepted-repeat case.
2. A setup work order covering monthly as well as catch-up keys, with honest exhaustion/restart semantics.
3. An explicit essential-admission guarantee or an explicit acceptance of classification-gate denial, with corresponding claims/tests.
4. Physical write admission for changed existing pages and deletion, plus a low-space cleanup transition.
5. Crash-safe attachment reset/publication and continuous quota ownership through Checking/Published.
6. A seven-day group-friend bootstrap representation, Ready responder retry/cache lifetime and asymmetric expiry rule.
7. Waiting-line success/credit/timeout/retry transitions.
8. Authoritative clock durability across carrier-only commits and permitted carrier recreation, or an explicitly accepted weaker guarantee.

Correct finding 9's conflicting implementation tests as well. The pinned-library recovery tests and dependency configurations must then validate the chosen contracts. Do not silently invent them during implementation. Full group-state derivation remains a valid reference; the optional shortcut must stay disabled until its equivalence test passes.

**Items that can stay:** copy counts, stamp difficulty, storage tiers/headroom, the 64 MB reserve, byte shares, rates, offer/queue times, range caps and the 256-record ceiling can remain clearly labelled starting values pending measurement. Their numerical tuning is not the reason for this hold. Restored-store rollback, copied devices, sponsor log completeness, sustained flooding, radio relaying that defeats physical-closeness inference and losses after expiry/eviction can remain stated limits. A chosen availability limit must be described honestly; it must not contradict a promised failure transition. Independent expert review before sensitive use remains separate and outstanding.

## Does the unwritten direct-transfer channel block the whole update?

**Only big attachments depend on that channel.** Its canonical week-bearing initiator frame, PSK info/prologue bytes, dispatch/admission, errors, `FILE_*` encodings and range exchange must be specified and reviewed before implementing big direct transfers. Those are genuine blockers for that feature, not a reason on their own to block mesh envelopes, labels, recognition display, pairing, group encryption, local storage or small carried attachments.

Small attachments use ordinary carrier envelopes and their manifest/file reassembly path. They still need finding 5 fixed, but need none of the unwritten direct-channel frames. Big group attachment propagation and its received confirmations do depend on completing direct transfer; recognition's optional transfer use must stay disabled until then. Deferring big transfers would need an explicit scope adjustment to the owner's single-update delivery plan, with truthful UI. The rest of the update remains held for the independent blockers above even if that scope adjustment is made.
